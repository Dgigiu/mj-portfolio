## Ready-to-use measures for busy kitchens

The Food Save app is a mobile tool designed to help professional kitchens reduce food waste with concrete, ready-to-use measures. Instead of abstract sustainability advice, it gives head chefs and kitchen managers a curated library of actions they can implement step by step. The focus is on practicality: busy teams quickly find measures that fit their context and integrate them into daily work.

## Role

I designed Food Save at Foodways, the Swiss food sustainability company behind MyFoodways, for United Against Waste, a Swiss non-profit. I worked with Moritz and Markus, who ran the project at Foodways, and with PM João Almeida in Lisbon. As the only designer on the project, I defined the user flows, designed the interface, and prepared handoffs for development. When João went on paternity leave, I covered the product work too, for about three months that spanned most of the build, and the team asked me to see the project through to the end.

## Food waste in a low-margin business

Food waste is an environmental and ethical problem, and in a low-margin business like gastronomy, an economic one too. United Against Waste works with companies to fight it, and Foodways was commissioned to create a mobile tool that brings actionable measures directly to professional kitchens.

Kitchen managers knew they should reduce waste, but didn’t know how to do it practically. Busy schedules made environmental motivation insufficient on its own. What they needed were concrete, implementable ideas that fit their fast-paced reality.

**Constraints:** fixed UAW visual identity, external dev team across time zones, tight pre-approved budget. Designs had to be focused and realistic to build.

## Designing for the decision-makers

We designed for head chefs and kitchen managers, not the whole kitchen: they decided which measures to adopt and communicated them to their teams. The goal was not to put another app in everyone’s hands in the kitchen, but to support decision-makers with clear, usable guidance they could translate into procedures.

I interviewed three people from hotel and restaurant kitchens, starting with paper mockups. A few patterns stood out:

- Many managers felt they were “probably wasting more than they should” but didn’t have a clear overview or plan;
- They were not interested in social features or competition; they cared about simple measures they could realistically introduce;
- Any solution had to be fast to consult and easy to revisit; they didn’t have the time or mental space for complex interfaces.

## From competition to practical tool

We dropped the competition between kitchens that the concept started with, because the managers we talked to didn’t want it. The original idea had teams implement measures and publicly share their results. On paper, this sounded engaging. In practice, managers did not see themselves using a competitive app or spending time publishing results. They wanted knowledge and concrete suggestions, not the social layer.

Given our limited budget, investing in social and competitive features would have meant less time and money for the core experience. I shared these insights with the Swiss team, and together we shifted the concept from a “food challenge” app to a Food Save app focused on:

- Providing a library of concrete waste-reducing measures;
- Helping managers choose measures that fit their reality;
- Supporting implementation with a simple, focused workflow.

It was a clearer, smaller product, and a better fit for what managers actually told us they wanted.

## A library kitchens could act on

### Measure library

I designed the measure library around familiar mobile patterns, so it would feel natural on both iOS and Android from a single shared build. The Swiss team provided the content: a structured set of measures and categories grounded in their expertise and the Food Save methodology. My role was to design how this information would work on a phone and be navigated in a busy work context.

Users could browse by category and quickly see which measures might be relevant to their kitchen type. Each measure was presented in a compact, scannable way, focusing on what to do, how to do it, and, where relevant, extra details such as examples or recipe ideas to use up specific ingredients. Each measure could also be shared directly with a colleague, so an idea worth trying wasn’t stuck with whoever happened to find it first.

### To-do list for implementation

I proposed spending the development budget that dropping the competition freed up on a checklist that supported implementation directly.

Instead of just reading measures and hoping to remember them, kitchen managers could:

- Select measures they wanted to implement;
- Store them in a simple to-do list inside the app;
- Mark them as completed once they had been introduced in the kitchen.

This small addition turned the app from a static library into a light planning tool. It fit the way managers work: they could collect ideas, prioritize them, and return when they had time to act.

### Home

Home gave returning users somewhere live to open to, instead of sending them straight back to a huge list of measures. From the first release, it combined two kinds of content: measures suggested from what a manager had already completed, and collections the content team curated by hand for timely or seasonal ideas.

### Branding and UI

I followed United Against Waste’s branding, as defined in the primary color, the presence of their logo, and the image style. Beyond that, the app’s visual system, including logo, layout, and detailed screen designs, was created by me, balancing consistency with clarity and readability across both platforms.

## Stepping in as interim PM

While covering for João, I recommended a single shared build over native iOS and Android apps. I had sent the same brief to several studios to pressure-test our options. One made a strong case for native builds, and as a designer I wanted to push for it, since native usually gives the better feel: speed, system integration, the small things users notice.

But the performance gain was too small to justify the cost and timeline, and a single shared build was the only way to ship on time and on budget. I brought the Swiss team my plan: a shared build by Brief, the studio that had built MyFoodways, since our shared understanding of the Swiss and multi-platform requirements still made them the right fit. They approved it.

I kept the developers moving, reported to stakeholders, and held my design work in parallel, working asynchronously with the Swiss team and with João through annotated Sketch files and written specs. It worked because João and I got on well, and because I put in the effort to keep everyone in the loop rather than treating the cover as someone else’s job.

## Part of a pair

Alongside Food Save, I designed the Waste Tracker, a companion app where kitchen staff logged the weight of the food they threw out at the end of each day. United Against Waste launched the two together in June 2019 as the Food Save Starter Kit: one app for ideas, the other for accountability. Food Save suggested measures to adopt; the Waste Tracker showed whether waste actually dropped after putting them in place.

Where Food Save and MyFoodways were text and image driven, the Waste Tracker was a data product, and most of its setup happened off the screen. Printable labels reused the app’s colors and icons, so the link between each bin and its category on screen was obvious. A setup kit in German, French, and English briefed each kitchen’s Waste Tracker Lead and the rest of the team. Kitchen logistics didn’t always allow a device by the bins, so each briefing also came in a version for a paper form the lead entered into the app later.

In the app, a one-off check came first: labeled containers, a set of scales, and a briefed team. From there, staff entered weights and saw their own visualizations. A password-protected web report let them share the same views with managers, and the Insights tab pointed back to Food Save, recommending measures for the categories a kitchen wasted most. I owned the full flows, the data visualizations, and the design of the setup kit.

## Smaller, and better for it

Once Food Save was running in real kitchens, the feedback confirmed the core bet. A bakery professional who used it, Jens Jung, put it plainly: the information and suggestions were “clear and easy to understand.” That was the whole aim, to turn a broad sustainability goal into steps a busy kitchen could grasp and act on.

I didn’t see usage or waste data after launch, so I can’t say how many kitchens used the Starter Kit or whether their waste dropped. Food Save was eventually discontinued. Like a lot of commissioned sustainability work, its lifespan was tied to the program behind it rather than its own usage.

### Reflections

Food Save ended up smaller than the idea it started from, and better for it. The competition was the more exciting pitch, but the kitchens we talked to wanted guidance they could use, and rebuilding the concept around that was the decision everything else depended on. Covering for João was the real stretch. For a few months I took on the budget, coordinated with an external studio, and kept everyone in sync so the release went out on time. By the end, I knew I could do that job alongside my own.
