## Everyone sees the latest version

Team Files connects Jira and Confluence to cloud storage providers like Dropbox and SharePoint, so teams can attach and edit files without creating endless duplicates. Users link files and folders directly from their cloud drives and open them in a click. Updates happen in the original storage, so everyone sees the latest version.

A normal Confluence attachment shows as a small static thumbnail, frozen at the moment it was uploaded and impossible to edit without downloading it first. Team Files replaces that with a full preview inline, one click away from edit mode, and the preview keeps updating as the file changes, whether that happens in Team Files or back in the original storage.

Team Files is made by ikuTeam and sold on the Atlassian Marketplace. ikuTeam grew out of TOPDOX, a previous startup I co-founded, where we learned that individual users valued what we had built but wouldn’t pay for it, while companies would. We carried that one idea forward, helping teams with the work around their documents. ikuTeam was bootstrapped at first and later backed by Atlassian Ventures.

## Role

I was one of three founders and the only one working as a designer from 2018 to 2025. Until 2020 I was at ikuTeam part-time, alongside my work at Foodways, and full-time from 2021. During that time, our team grew to seven people. I handled UX and UI, branding, communication, UX writing, and later, product documentation, shaping the product from initial flows and visual identity to the design system, handoff, and ongoing improvements.

I wasn’t the only one who thought like a designer, though: our product owner, another co-founder, had a design background and pushed back on my work when it needed it. And every flow proposal went to our CTO, the third co-founder, before it went into a spec: whether it was feasible, what the spec needed to cover, what could wait. That meant technical limits surfaced before build, not partway through it.

## Download, edit, re-upload, repeat

Once someone attached a document to a Jira issue or Confluence page, collaboration became a cycle of downloading, editing, and re-uploading. Every kind of team in Jira and Confluence, from software to support, marketing, and operations, ended up cluttering pages with copies and losing track of which file was “the latest.” Cloud storage providers already solved collaboration, but the integration with Atlassian tools was weak. Our goal was to bring cloud files into Jira and Confluence in a way that respected how these teams actually worked: one source of truth, one-click access, and no extra friction.

From the start, our strategy was to do more than just let users paste a link. We aimed to build a deeper integration between Atlassian and cloud drives: connecting storage accounts and folders to specific projects, attaching files directly to issues and pages, and enabling one-click edit while keeping files in their original storage. Above all, we wanted the product to feel like a native extension of Jira and Confluence, visually and behaviorally.

Browser security shaped the experience too. Opening a file’s folder in its cloud storage couldn’t use a new tab, so it replaced the Jira or Confluence page, and closing the tab out of habit lost that page. Some users complained, and I shared the frustration, but the alternative was not having that option at all.

## The 2023 UX review

In 2023 I reviewed the whole product against Nielsen Norman’s usability heuristics, wrote up a report, turned each finding into a user story in Jira, and worked through them sprint by sprint rather than treating the review as a one-time cleanup. By then the main flows were stable, and we’d shifted from just making it work to making it smooth. The goal was to find what could be clearer, easier to use, and more accessible.

A few of those changes stuck with me. Some actions weren’t visible enough, so I added a dedicated preview button to the file options, since many people never realized they could open a full-screen preview. I also gave every file and folder action its own icon, so they could be scanned at a glance. We added contextual help, with each part of the app linking to the relevant documentation right where you needed it. And we rewrote error messages so each one guided the user toward a resolution, with no more “something went wrong” dead ends.

It’s also when I started pulling more of Atlassian’s own design system into the product: metadata labels on files and folders got a size increase for legibility, and I revised colors across the board to meet its contrast requirements. No single change was dramatic on its own. But work like this, alongside steady improvements to stability and usability, is the kind of thing that quietly compounds into a product people stay with.

## What long tenure lets you notice

### Attaching files, redesigned many times

Over the years I redesigned the core attach flow many times, to simplify the steps and clarify the language. It was always the biggest challenge: attaching a file had to be more powerful than a simple link but still feel straightforward, and first-time users had to connect a cloud account, connect a folder to a project, then select files to attach. One addition along the way: a live preview showed how attachments would look on the page before you attached them.

### One step less to connect a folder

When no storage was connected yet, I had “connect folder” take people straight to connecting one, then land them right back in the flow to select folders. Before, the same path dropped them into an empty list of connected storages. One step less. It held up in our internal testing, and once it was live, it didn’t draw complaints.

The path to making Team Files better was built from moments like this, the kind of friction you only notice once you’ve watched enough people hit it over time. That noticing is something long tenure on a product buys you that a short engagement never can.

### A history for every connected folder

I designed a log for each connected folder, and the need for it came from support, one of the best places to do that noticing. For a while, a recurring ticket had users suddenly losing access to connected folders. Engineering kept making the code more resilient, and that helped, but every new ticket still meant piecing together what had happened. Was it a real error, or had someone disconnected the folder and connected it again? At the time, reconnecting could break the links to files already attached. So I raised it with our CTO and product owner, and the log let admins see each folder’s history and tell one case from the other. After it shipped, that kind of ticket became rare, and when one did come in, we had a clear record to work from.

### Empty states as onboarding

When we dropped the separate Team Files sign-up that the app’s first version required, I moved the explaining into the product itself: empty states that guided users toward connecting their first folder, and a short tour of the file manager.

Dropping the sign-up was a real engineering achievement, and a strategic call as much as a design one. It simplified access for everyone, especially the large share of users who only wanted to view attached files and previously had to create an account just to look. But it removed an important moment to explain the product. Most users never chose to install Team Files; their admin did. Usually, the first they saw of it was a file someone had attached to an issue, which they could open right away or unlock by connecting their own cloud account.

The ones who went looking for the app itself, through the Jira apps menu, landed in a file manager with nothing connected yet. That first-run empty state became the real onboarding, not a placeholder, and it reinforced something for me: when you can’t control where someone enters a product, every default state has to teach.

### Shared folders, one account per person

As customers grew, we let admins choose how shared folders worked: simple sharing, or each person signing in with their own cloud account. Team Files started with small teams, so sharing a folder was simple: when one person connected it, everyone else browsed it through that person’s cloud account. At larger companies, that stopped being enough. Admins raised it as a security risk, and it broke accountability, since edits to shared Word documents all appeared under the name of whoever had shared the folder. Many small teams still wanted the simple version, so we added the choice instead of replacing it.

The design challenge was adding that step without making shared folders feel broken. I kept folders visible with a clear Authenticate action, let people reuse an account they had already connected, and made a failed attempt offer another account instead of a dead end. Over time, signing in with your own account became the default.

### Built for narrow panels

I planned layouts so the app stayed usable in a small side panel in Jira and a wide full-page view alike. Team Files isn’t a mobile app, but it carries many of the same constraints: tight panels, a wide range of screen sizes, and information that has to be prioritized so the most important things survive when space runs short. In narrow views, attachments dropped secondary metadata first, and the file manager grouped its folder actions under a single button, giving the space to the files themselves while keeping every action one click away.

## A design system engineers built from

Alongside the product work, I established the design system. It started as Team Files components extending the Atlassian Design System Figma library to speed up my own prototyping and keep the experience coherent across Jira, Confluence, and ikuTeam’s other apps. Over time it outgrew the design side. When the team started preparing the Forge rebuild, engineers built a Storybook from it: anything headed for the new Forge foundation was built there first, straight from the Figma library. My role shifted from author to providing direction. A system that crosses from Figma into the codebase is the point where it stops being a designer’s artifact and starts being shared infrastructure. That shift also meant more time with developers on UI and UX questions, and more time making the case that consistency wasn’t polish but part of product quality.

I also moved from Sketch to Figma, and improved handoffs with interactive prototypes and detailed Jira tickets that doubled as the basis for user documentation. Later, AI joined the toolkit too. I used ChatGPT to draft first versions of UI messages, then rewrote them to fit the product’s voice and context, and used image generation for the illustrations in our blog posts about Team Files. It was early days for these tools, so the use was limited, but it set the habit I still follow: let the tool get a first draft on the page, and keep the decisions mine.

## Beyond the product

Outside the app, I ran the most experiments on the marketing site, which I designed, wrote, and coded, and on the Marketplace listing: taglines, keywords, and call-to-action variants like “Get started (it’s free)” and “Free trial,” all aimed at getting people from the site to the Marketplace and into a trial. We tracked clicks from each button to the Marketplace. Saying it was free to start seemed to help.

The work also reached back into the product. We didn’t have our users’ email addresses, so a newsletter wasn’t an option for announcing new features. The What’s new screen was my idea for filling that gap inside the app, with each feature linked to the blog post that explained it.

## Keeping it coherent while it grows

Team Files launched several years ago and is still actively maintained. It has over 9,000 active installations and was rated 3.7 out of 4 on the Atlassian Marketplace during my tenure. Churn tells the other half of the story. Atlassian shared churn as an index against the Marketplace average, where 1 meant average. When we started tracking it to see where we were losing customers, we were close to 3. We made bringing it down a yearly KPI, and by the time I left it sat around 1, sometimes 1.1 and at times just under. That was a team effort across engineering, support, documentation, and design, and I can’t pin it on any one change, but it’s the clearest sign that the steady work described above did what it was meant to. The core bet itself was confirmed by the people using it day to day:

<Quote attribution="Frank Leclerc, Decadis" source="Atlassian Marketplace review, 2023">

“Team Files is the perfect way to connect the file-based world with Confluence. There are always documents that are created or received in file form. With Team Files, these don’t have to be uploaded to Confluence as attachments, but can be located in a cloud service and elegantly integrated into Confluence. For us, the app is a very helpful tool.”

</Quote>

Most design work is measured in sprints. Team Files gave me something rarer: the same product and the same core problem to keep getting right, with a user base that grew year after year. That length changed how I designed. I could watch a decision age, see where my own past choices created friction, and fix them with the benefit of hindsight instead of guesswork. It also meant the work was never just one product. Keeping the experience coherent across Jira and Confluence, and across ikuTeam’s other apps like the Office Editor, was a constant background effort that doesn’t show up in any single screen. Owning one product that long taught me that the hard part isn’t shipping a good first version. It’s keeping it coherent while it grows.
