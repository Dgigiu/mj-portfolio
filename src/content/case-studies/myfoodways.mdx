## Cooking from what’s in the fridge

MyFoodways was a sustainable recipes app for iOS and Android designed to help people cook healthy, practical meals with the ingredients they already had in the fridge. The core scenario: it’s 7 PM on a weekday, fatigue sets in, and people default to familiar meals. MyFoodways suggested recipes based on what was on hand, removing the need for an extra trip to the store or tricky substitutions. The aim was to make sustainable cooking feel like a small choice in an everyday routine.

## Role

I joined Foodways, a Swiss food sustainability company, in March 2018, shortly before the first public release, after the initial prototype and visual style were established. Working closely with PM Laura Robinson in Switzerland, the local Foodways team, and the external engineering team in Lisbon, I was the only designer on the project through December 2020, handling user flows, interface design, and developer handoff.

## Recipes that don’t fit real life

Swiss authorities commissioned MyFoodways to help households reduce food waste with a mobile-first experience, as both a public service and a behavior-change experiment. The real challenge: people wanted to cook sustainably but faced decision fatigue at day’s end, and most recipes didn’t fit their lives. It’s a pattern still common in recipe apps today: rigid ingredient lists with no room to improvise, and portions fixed regardless of who’s actually cooking.

With roughly three months between my start in March and a hard public launch in June 2018, we cut scope deliberately, holding back features like the recipe library for after launch, and kept adding recipes throughout the app’s first year live. The other constraints were just as fixed: a visual identity from an external agency, with a pastel-heavy palette that predated any of the accessibility work, and an outsourced dev team across time zones. Our goal was to make sustainable cooking a minor adjustment, not a lifestyle overhaul.

## Making the mission practical

Research set the app’s priorities: ingredient-led discovery first, flexible recipes second, lightweight planning third. User research and early focus groups confirmed the problem: people loved the sustainability mission but needed practicality.

Key findings:

- People wanted easy recipes using what was in their fridge right now;
- Portion scaling and ingredient swaps were essential for small households and flexible cooking;
- Sustainable options had to feel familiar, with small tweaks to everyday meals rather than radical changes.

When I joined, MyFoodways already had a clear “fridge-first” vision and chef-curated recipes. What it needed were behavioral enablers: intuitive ingredient swapping and portion scaling. We avoided didactic messages and focused on tools that nudged choices in a sustainable direction, keeping behavior change user-driven and low-barrier.

## Search, profile, recipes and challenges

### 1. Ingredient-focused search

Search started with “What’s in your fridge?” Users entered ingredients (spinach, feta, pesto) and received recipe combinations instantly. This gave immediate meal ideas without waste. Seasonal, quick-meal, and dietary filters narrowed options. Advanced filters stayed tucked away to avoid overwhelming beginners.

### 2. Profile personalization

The profile wasn’t a one-time setup. It was the place the app went back to every time it decided what to show. Onboarding asked three short questions: diet (whether the person eats meat, fish, and dairy, and how often), motivation (spend less time cooking, eat seasonally, or avoid food waste), and allergies.

The answers became settings in the personalization tab that the person could update as their needs changed, alongside household composition. Diet and no-gos filtered the catalog, motivations shaped what got surfaced, and household composition set the default portion scale so recipes arrived sized for the right number of people.

<Figure
  src={personalization}
  alt="Profile screen showing ‘My no-gos’ personalization."
  caption='“My no-gos” replaced “food allergies”, making the profile accessible to everyone, not only medical contexts.'
/>

No-gos did more than hide things. If a recipe used an ingredient someone avoided but a safe alternative existed, the recipe still appeared, with the alternative already swapped in rather than dropped from the results. Add soy to your no-gos and a recipe that called for it would quietly show up with something else in its place. The catalog stayed open instead of shrinking every time someone added a restriction. This is the automatic, profile-driven half of a swap system that also had a manual side inside each recipe, covered below.

In the first update after launch, prompted by user feedback, we reworded the allergies screen: the onboarding question became “are there any ingredients you’d rather avoid,” and the setting became “My no-gos.” Framing it as allergies had turned a cooking preference into a medical declaration, heavier than it needed to be for people who simply disliked an ingredient or avoided it for reasons that had nothing to do with health. The softer framing covered a serious allergy and a personal dislike equally, without asking anyone to categorize themselves medically.

### 3. Flexible recipe flow

Every recipe loaded with smart defaults from the user’s profile:

- **Portion scaling** automatically adjusted to household size (for example “2 adults, 0 kids”) with instant recalculation, making cooking for the right number easier;
- **Ingredient swaps** appeared via yellow chevrons (spinach → chard → kale), the manual, in-the-moment side of the swap system: out of spinach tonight, tap to see what else works. The alternatives still respected your no-gos. It doubled as a confidence aid for less experienced cooks, so a missing ingredient stopped being a reason to abandon the recipe or make an extra trip to the store;
- **“Let’s cook”** generated final cooking steps with the reviewed ingredients and quantities, plus leftover suggestions and storage tips to encourage sustainable habits.

<Figure

The “Let’s cook” button was a pragmatic MVP decision. Live, step-by-step cooking updates weren’t technically feasible for v1, so we consolidated everything into a single server call. This gave users one quick path from ingredient review to cooking.

### 4. Seven-day challenges (2020)

The last feature I worked on was a challenge format to nudge habits over a set period, run as a cohort rather than a self-serve menu: every user took it on at the same time, with an email and push notifications a few days ahead to build some anticipation.

The one that shipped was “Plant-based Rockstar,” a seven-day challenge built around small, doable tweaks to eat more vegetables and discover plant protein sources. Each day had a theme, like working lentils into dinner, with the app’s recipes or a companion post on the MyFoodways blog for ideas. Completing a day’s task let you check it off, and the app would offer a prewritten message you could share with friends through WhatsApp or your messages app, a light invitation rather than a competition. Some days also included an offer from a partner.

<Figure
  src={challenges}

I designed the full flow for this first challenge, with more planned to follow. The follow-ups were never greenlit, and the format was paused after the first run. I don’t have engagement data to point to, so I’ll only say what I know: one challenge shipped, the rest didn’t, and the idea didn’t become a recurring part of the app.

## Contrast versus a fixed brand

Wherever the fixed brand left me room, I fixed contrast, and where it didn’t, I flagged it. The visual identity arrived fixed from an external agency. Its first primary color, a soft pink, had its own legibility problems, and user feedback pushed the team toward yellow instead. I got the revised brand guidelines when I joined and worked on the switch to the bright yellow that signaled most of the interactive elements.

Initially, the CTA buttons had regular white text over the primary color. I already suspected white on that particular yellow would be a stretch, so I made it bold and all-caps to improve legibility. At the same time, the recipe tags moved from filled color badges with white text to a dot next to dark text, easier to read and taking up less space. The tab bar’s selected state moved from the soft pink to dark gray before v1 shipped too, defensible both on the general look of iOS apps at the time and on contrast. Three small calls, one instinct.

None of that was enough for the buttons, though. Once live, bright yellow (`#FAD21E`) sat behind primary action buttons with white text on top, and white on that yellow scored 1.47:1. The AA threshold for large text is 3:1, so this wasn’t a borderline call. I flagged it and proposed alternatives, a darker toasted orange first, then other colors pulled straight from the brand guide. All were turned down. The palette was the agency’s to defend, and the yellow had just won a round on user feedback, so it wasn’t moving again. It stayed survivable for a while, only a handful of buttons, always saying the same thing, “Sign in” and “Let’s cook,” so people learned them by shape and position rather than reading them.

<Figure
  src={accessibility}
  alt="Comparison of the Beta and Version 1.0 recipe screens showing the button, tag, and tab bar treatments."
  caption="Quick fixes landed everywhere except the buttons themselves."
/>

In the first real update after launch, I went through the app for legibility more broadly: section headers went from a light weight to properly dark, cards got more room to breathe, and the ingredient-swap indicator got a more prominent look to communicate it was interactive, among other smaller fixes. The bigger repeat of the same instinct was the Favourites screen: a segmented control became a familiar tab look, dark text signaling the selected tab like the app’s own tab bar, yellow reduced to reinforcement rather than the label’s fill.

<Figure
  src={postLaunch}
  alt="Comparison of the Version 1.0 and First Update onboarding and Favourites screens."
  caption="More fixes followed, this time after launch."
/>

The Challenges feature, in the last update, finally broke the standing workaround. Its buttons carried more text, and the text changed from screen to screen, so there was nothing fixed left to learn. Bad contrast on a predictable label is one thing. Bad contrast on a label you have to actually read every time is another. That gave me the case to push harder, this time on a new surface rather than against the locked core palette. I designed the challenge buttons as white with an emboss so they still read as tappable, used a warm gray (`#5B5752`) for the label, which cleared AA, and put the brand yellow to work as a state: a button filled yellow once you completed that day’s task. Yellow went from the thing hurting legibility to a signal that meant something.

It was a contained win. The new pattern applied to Challenges, while the original buttons kept their treatment.

## Scoping honestly across time zones

We scoped pragmatically: “Let’s cook” was the simple solution, and “Favourites cookbook” waited until after launch. I worked mostly asynchronously with PM Laura Robinson, switching to formal syncs with the Lisbon team and developers for key milestones. Handoffs used annotated Sketch files and Laura’s written specs for amendments, and that documentation kept the work moving even when Laura was away.

## Good design isn’t enough on its own

### Impact

The app reached over 6,000 downloads in its first six months, around 40% of them in Switzerland. What stood out in user feedback was that people described the product back in the same terms we had designed around. One five-star review put it plainly:

> “A great cookbook that lets you substitute ingredients in the recipe so you can use whatever you have in the fridge.”

The fridge-first premise and ingredient swapping, the two bets the whole experience rested on, were exactly what users named when they talked about why they liked it. Engagement decreased over time as sourcing chef content became unsustainable, and after public funding concluded the app transitioned to maintenance mode.

### Reflections

MyFoodways taught me that good design isn’t enough on its own. The app did what it set out to do, but sustainability work needs a business model behind it, and public funding left no room for one. When the money ran out, so did the chef-curated content the experience depended on. What stuck with me was everything around the screens: pushing on constraints I couldn’t always move, scoping honestly across time zones, and knowing how to read it when a bet didn’t land. I came out of it a more patient designer, and a more confident cook.
